# Columbia ᗒ□ᗕvᗑᐃ⟋ ### The Philosophy of Mathematics of Representation **Columbia ᗒ□ᗕvᗑᐃ⟋** begins with a different question from the other mathematical philosophies: > **How does a mathematical structure become representable?** Its central thesis is: $$ \boxed{\text{Mathematics is the study of structures through their representations and transformations.}} $$ The symbols represent six fundamental movements: $$ \boxed{ \mathsf{ᗒ} \rightarrow \Box \rightarrow \mathsf{ᗕ} \rightarrow v \rightarrow \mathsf{ᗑ} \rightarrow \mathsf{ᐃ} \rightarrow \mathsf{\⟋} } $$ --- # I. ᗒ — Representation Every mathematical object must appear in some representational form. A number may appear as $$ 7, $$ as $$ 111_2, $$ as $$ VII, $$ or as $$ 3+4. $$ The representations differ. The mathematical object does not necessarily change. Therefore: $$ \boxed{\text{Representation} \neq \text{Reality}.} $$ The first philosophical problem of mathematics is consequently the relationship between an object and its representation. --- # II. □ — The Formal Boundary The square, $$ \Box, $$ represents a formal system. A mathematical theory establishes a boundary: $$ \mathcal F=\{\text{permitted expressions, rules, objects, and transformations}\}. $$ Inside the boundary, certain operations are meaningful. Outside it, they may not be defined. Thus a formal system is not merely a collection of axioms. It is a **space of legitimate mathematical operations**. --- # III. ᗕ — Interpretation A formal expression acquires mathematical meaning through interpretation. Suppose we have $$ x+y=z. $$ The symbols alone do not determine whether \(+\) represents: * ordinary addition, * matrix addition, * polynomial addition, * vector addition, * addition in a finite group. Interpretation maps syntax into structure: $$ \boxed{ \text{Syntax} \xrightarrow{\;\mathcal I\;} \text{Semantics}. } $$ Therefore: $$ \boxed{\text{Meaning is a mapping from form to structure.}} $$ --- # IV. \(v\) — Information The symbol \(v\) represents information flowing through a mathematical structure. A mathematical transformation $$ f:X\rightarrow Y $$ carries information from one representation to another. Some transformations preserve information: $$ x_1\neq x_2 \Rightarrow f(x_1)\neq f(x_2). $$ Others collapse distinctions: $$ x_1\neq x_2 \quad\text{but}\quad f(x_1)=f(x_2). $$ Columbia ᗒ□ᗕvᗑᐃ⟋ therefore treats **information preservation and information loss** as fundamental mathematical concepts. --- # V. ᗑ — Compression A mathematical abstraction compresses many concrete representations into a smaller conceptual structure. For example: $$ 2+2=4, $$ $$ 3+3=6, $$ $$ 10+10=20 $$ can all be instances of the abstract operation $$ x+x=2x. $$ Abstraction therefore performs a form of structural compression: $$ \boxed{ \text{Many Instances} \longrightarrow \text{One Structure}. } $$ The best abstractions preserve what matters while discarding irrelevant representation. --- # VI. ᐃ — Expansion Compression has a dual operation. A single abstract structure can generate many concrete instances: $$ \boxed{ \text{One Structure} \longrightarrow \text{Many Representations}. } $$ For example, $$ f(x)=x^2 $$ can generate $$ f(1)=1, $$ $$ f(2)=4, $$ $$ f(3)=9, $$ and infinitely many others. Thus mathematical thought oscillates between: $$ \text{compression} \leftrightarrow \text{expansion}. $$ --- # VII. ⟋ — Transformation The final symbol represents movement between representations. A transformation $$ T:A\rightarrow B $$ changes the form in which mathematical information appears. The central question is: > **What survives the transformation?** If a property \(P\) satisfies $$ P(x)\Rightarrow P(T(x)), $$ then \(P\) is preserved. Thus transformation allows us to distinguish essential mathematical structure from accidental representation. --- # VIII. The Representation Principle Columbia ᗒ□ᗕvᗑᐃ⟋ proposes: $$ \boxed{ \text{A mathematical object is inseparable from the space of its valid representations.} } $$ But it does **not** identify an object with any particular representation. Instead: $$ X \leftrightarrow \{R_1(X),R_2(X),R_3(X),\ldots\}. $$ The object is understood through the relationships among its representations. --- # IX. Equivalence Two representations \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) may be mathematically equivalent: $$ R_1\sim R_2. $$ This relation can be formalized through an equivalence relation satisfying: $$ R\sim R, $$ $$ R_1\sim R_2\Rightarrow R_2\sim R_1, $$ and $$ R_1\sim R_2,\quad R_2\sim R_3 \Rightarrow R_1\sim R_3. $$ A mathematical object can then be viewed as an equivalence class: $$ [X]=\{R:R\sim X\}. $$ This makes equivalence one of the fundamental mechanisms by which mathematics moves beyond notation. --- # X. Proof as Translation A proof can be interpreted as a sequence of valid translations: $$ R_0 \rightarrow R_1 \rightarrow R_2 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow R_n. $$ Each step changes representation while preserving validity. Therefore: $$ \boxed{ \text{Proof} = \text{controlled transformation of mathematical information}. } $$ This connects proof with computation. A proof and a program can both be understood as structured transformations. --- # XI. Computation A program is a representation transformer: $$ P:X\rightarrow Y. $$ The source representation is transformed into a target representation. For functional programming: $$ f:x\mapsto y. $$ For a compiler: $$ \text{Source Code} \rightarrow \text{Intermediate Representation} \rightarrow \text{Machine Code}. $$ For mathematics: $$ \text{Concept} \rightarrow \text{Formal Expression} \rightarrow \text{Proof}. $$ Columbia ᗒ□ᗕvᗑᐃ⟋ therefore treats computation as a special case of mathematical representation transformation. --- # XII. Language Mathematical language is not merely a communication device. It constrains what can easily be expressed. A language \(L\) defines a set of expressions: $$ \operatorname{Expr}(L). $$ A richer language can make previously obscure structures directly expressible. Thus: $$ \boxed{ \text{Changing mathematical language can change the space of discoverable structure.} } $$ This does not mean that changing notation changes mathematical truth. It means that representation affects **what humans can see and manipulate efficiently**. --- # XIII. The Mathematics of Information Columbia ᗒ□ᗕvᗑᐃ⟋ gives special importance to the question: $$ \text{How much structure survives?} $$ For a transformation $$ T:X\rightarrow Y, $$ we can investigate: $$ \ker(T), $$ $$ \operatorname{im}(T), $$ and the information encoded in the fibers $$ T^{-1}(y). $$ A transformation therefore has two complementary aspects: $$ \boxed{ \text{What it preserves} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \text{what it forgets}. } $$ --- # XIV. Mathematical Discovery Discovery occurs when a new representation makes an existing structure visible. For example, a difficult problem may become simple after changing coordinates: $$ x,y \rightarrow r,\theta. $$ The underlying mathematical reality has not necessarily changed. The representation has. Yet the new representation can expose hidden structure. Thus: $$ \boxed{ \text{Discovery often consists of finding the representation in which the invariant becomes obvious.} } $$ --- # XV. The Columbia Cycle The entire philosophy can be represented as: $$ \boxed{ \text{Represent} \rightarrow \text{Formalize} \rightarrow \text{Interpret} \rightarrow \text{Transform} \rightarrow \text{Compress} \rightarrow \text{Expand} \rightarrow \text{Represent again} } $$ This creates a continuous mathematical loop: $$ R_0 \rightarrow R_1 \rightarrow R_2 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow R_n \sim R_0. $$ The mathematician repeatedly changes representations until the underlying structure becomes transparent. --- # XVI. The Central Law The philosophy can ultimately be compressed into: $$ \boxed{ \text{Meaning} = \text{Structure preserved across representations}. } $$ And its deepest principle is: > **“Change the representation; preserve the structure.”** In symbolic form: $$ \boxed{ R_1 \xrightarrow{\;T\;} R_2 \qquad \text{while} \qquad \operatorname{Inv}(R_1)=\operatorname{Inv}(R_2). } $$ **Columbia ᗒ□ᗕvᗑᐃ⟋** therefore defines mathematics as the investigation of **how abstract structures are represented, formalized, interpreted, compressed, expanded, and transformed while their essential invariants remain intelligible.**
