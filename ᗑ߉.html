# MIT ᗑ߉ ### The Philosophy of Mathematics of Information **MIT ᗑ߉** begins with one proposition: > **Mathematics is the transformation of information under rules.** Everything else—numbers, functions, proofs, programs, geometries, simulations—can be understood as particular organizations of information. The philosophy is deliberately minimal. $$ \boxed{\mathsf{ᗑ}\quad\mathsf{߉}} $$ Two symbols. Two primitives. --- # I. ᗑ — Information The first primitive is **information**. A mathematical state is an element $$ x\in X. $$ The important question is not what \(x\) is made of, but what distinctions \(x\) contains. If $$ x\neq y, $$ then the distinction $$ x\mid y $$ is information. Thus: $$ \boxed{ \text{Information}=\text{mathematically meaningful distinction}. } $$ --- # II. ߉ — Transformation The second primitive is transformation: $$ f:X\rightarrow Y. $$ A transformation changes the organization of information. Therefore the fundamental mathematical event is: $$ \boxed{ x\xrightarrow{f}y. } $$ Everything else is structure built around this relation. --- # III. The MIT Principle MIT ᗑ߉ proposes: $$ \boxed{ \text{Mathematics} = \text{Information} + \text{Transformation}. } $$ A number is information. Addition is transformation. A set is organized information. A function is a transformation. A proof is a sequence of valid transformations. A program is an executable transformation. A physical simulation is a transformation of states. --- # IV. Objects Are States An object is a state of information: $$ x\in X. $$ But an isolated state tells us little. Its meaning comes from the transformations available to it: $$ \operatorname{Trans}(x). $$ Thus: $$ \boxed{ \text{Object} = \text{state within a transformation system}. } $$ --- # V. Functions Are Fundamental MIT ᗑ߉ treats functions as more fundamental than static objects. Instead of beginning with $$ x, $$ begin with $$ f:x\mapsto y. $$ A function establishes a relationship between informational states. Composition then becomes fundamental: $$ X\xrightarrow{f}Y\xrightarrow{g}Z $$ gives $$ g\circ f:X\rightarrow Z. $$ Thus complicated mathematics can be constructed from simple transformations. --- # VI. Composition The fundamental operation of MIT ᗑ߉ is composition. Given $$ f:X\rightarrow Y $$ and $$ g:Y\rightarrow Z, $$ we construct: $$ g\circ f:X\rightarrow Z. $$ This creates a hierarchy: $$ f_1, \quad f_2, \quad f_2\circ f_1, \quad f_3\circ f_2\circ f_1, \ldots $$ Complex mathematical processes emerge from compositions of simple ones. Therefore: $$ \boxed{ \text{Complexity} = \text{composition of transformations}. } $$ --- # VII. Computation A computation is simply a sequence: $$ x_0 \rightarrow x_1 \rightarrow x_2 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow x_n. $$ Each arrow represents a transformation. Thus: $$ \boxed{ \text{Computation} = \text{organized transformation of information}. } $$ A computer is therefore a machine for realizing mathematical transformations. --- # VIII. Compression Information can be represented compactly. A large structure $$ X $$ may be generated from a much smaller description $$ D. $$ If $$ D\rightarrow X, $$ then \(D\) is a compressed description of \(X\). MIT ᗑ߉ therefore treats abstraction as a form of compression: $$ \boxed{ \text{Abstraction} = \text{short description preserving relevant structure}. } $$ --- # IX. Algorithms An algorithm is a finite specification of transformations. $$ A: x_0\rightarrow x_1\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow x_n. $$ The algorithm is mathematical structure made operational. Two algorithms can compute the same function: $$ A\equiv B, $$ while having different computational costs. Therefore MIT ᗑ߉ distinguishes: $$ \boxed{ \text{What can be computed} } $$ from $$ \boxed{ \text{How efficiently it can be computed}. } $$ --- # X. Complexity Once information and transformation are fundamental, complexity becomes unavoidable. For an algorithm \(A\), define a resource measure: $$ C(A,n). $$ This might describe: * time, * memory, * communication, * number of operations, * approximation error. The philosophy therefore asks not only: $$ \text{“Is }f\text{ computable?”} $$ but: $$ \boxed{ \text{“What does it cost to compute }f\text{?”} } $$ --- # XI. Information Loss A transformation can preserve distinctions: $$ x\neq y \Rightarrow f(x)\neq f(y), $$ or destroy them: $$ x\neq y \quad\text{but}\quad f(x)=f(y). $$ MIT ᗑ߉ calls the latter **information collapse**. A transformation can therefore be analyzed through: $$ \boxed{ \text{preservation} \quad\leftrightarrow\quad \text{loss}. } $$ This gives a common mathematical language for compression, projection, hashing, quotient structures, measurement, and abstraction. --- # XII. Proof A proof is a valid transformation of information. Starting from assumptions: $$ \Gamma, $$ we transform them through a sequence $$ \Gamma \rightarrow P_1 \rightarrow P_2 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow P. $$ The theorem is the resulting informational state. Thus: $$ \boxed{ \text{Proof} = \text{verified transformation}. } $$ --- # XIII. Mathematics and Programming MIT ᗑ߉ makes the boundary between mathematics and programming unusually thin. A mathematical function: $$ f:X\rightarrow Y $$ and a program: ```text f : X -> Y ``` can represent the same abstract transformation. The distinction becomes one of interpretation and implementation rather than fundamental mathematical nature. Therefore: $$ \boxed{ \text{Program} = \text{implemented mathematical transformation}. } $$ --- # XIV. The Minimal Mathematical Universe MIT ᗑ߉ attempts to reconstruct mathematics from only two primitives: $$ \boxed{\mathsf{ᗑ}=\text{information}} $$ and $$ \boxed{\mathsf{߉}=\text{transformation}}. $$ From these we can construct: $$ \begin{aligned} \text{Objects} &\rightarrow \text{states}\\ \text{Functions} &\rightarrow \text{transformations}\\ \text{Proofs} &\rightarrow \text{verified transformations}\\ \text{Programs} &\rightarrow \text{executable transformations}\\ \text{Algorithms} &\rightarrow \text{composed transformations}\\ \text{Abstraction} &\rightarrow \text{compressed information}\\ \text{Complexity} &\rightarrow \text{cost of transformation}. \end{aligned} $$ --- # XV. The ᗑ߉ Law The entire philosophy can be expressed in one equation: $$ \boxed{ \mathsf{ᗑ} \xrightarrow{\;\mathsf{߉}\;} \mathsf{ᗑ}' } $$ Information becomes new information through transformation. Repeated: $$ \mathsf{ᗑ}_0 \xrightarrow{f_1} \mathsf{ᗑ}_1 \xrightarrow{f_2} \mathsf{ᗑ}_2 \xrightarrow{f_3} \cdots $$ Mathematics is the theory of these transformations. --- # XVI. The MIT Axiom **Axiom ᗑ߉:** > **Every mathematical process can be understood as a transformation of distinguishable information.** And conversely: > **Every sufficiently well-defined transformation of information constitutes a mathematical object of study.** --- # XVII. The MIT Motto The shortest expression of the philosophy is: $$ \boxed{\textbf{Information transforms.}} $$ Or: $$ \boxed{ \text{ᗑ}\rightarrow\text{߉}\rightarrow\text{ᗑ}' } $$ **MIT ᗑ߉** therefore defines mathematics as the study of **information, transformation, composition, computation, and the resources required to transform one mathematical state into another.**
