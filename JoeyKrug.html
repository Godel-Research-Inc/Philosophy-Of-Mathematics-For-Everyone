# Who Is Ancestral to Whom? ## Rethinking the Meaning of “Eurasian DNA” in Amazigh, Egyptian, Fulani, Somali, Ethiopian, and Eritrean Populations ### Abstract Population genetics frequently describes portions of African genomes as having “Eurasian,” “West Eurasian,” “Middle Eastern,” or “European” ancestry. These descriptions can be scientifically useful, but they also raise a deeper question about what an ancestry assignment actually establishes. If Homo sapiens originated in Africa and non-African populations ultimately descend from African populations that migrated beyond the continent, then the appearance of genetic similarity between an African population and a Eurasian reference population does not, by itself, establish a simple one-directional historical relationship in which “Eurasian ancestry entered Africa.” The same underlying genetic relationship may require a more complicated demographic explanation involving ancient African populations, subsequent Eurasian diversification, population separation, and later back-migration into Africa. This paper proposes a methodological challenge: **When a genetic test identifies “Eurasian ancestry” in an African population, how does the test establish the direction of ancestry rather than merely detecting shared ancestry?** The question is particularly relevant for Amazigh, Egyptian, Fulani, Somali, Ethiopian, and Eritrean populations, whose histories involve repeated movements across the Sahara, Nile Valley, Red Sea, Arabian Peninsula, Mediterranean, and wider African regions. The purpose of this paper is not to deny documented migration from Eurasia into Africa. Such migration is supported by multiple genetic and archaeological studies. Rather, the purpose is to distinguish three different propositions: 1. that two populations share ancestry; 2. that one population received gene flow from another; 3. that the shared ancestry ultimately originated in one geographic region rather than another. These are not identical claims. --- # 1. The Provocative Question Suppose a genetic analysis examines an Amazigh, Egyptian, Fulani, Somali, Ethiopian, or Eritrean genome and identifies a component that most closely resembles populations from Europe, the Levant, Arabia, or another part of western Eurasia. The conventional interpretation might be: > “This population possesses Eurasian ancestry.” But a more fundamental question is: > **What exactly does “Eurasian ancestry” mean, and what evidence determines the direction of the historical relationship?** Human evolution introduces an obvious complication. If the ancestors of non-African Homo sapiens originated in Africa, then every population outside Africa ultimately inherited ancestry from African populations. Consequently, the statement: > “Africans have Eurasian DNA” can be reframed as: > “Some African populations share genetic ancestry with populations currently classified as Eurasian.” Those statements sound similar, but they make very different historical claims. --- # 2. Shared Ancestry Is Not Automatically Gene-Flow Direction Genetic ancestry modeling is fundamentally comparative. Researchers compare genomes against reference populations, ancient genomes, statistical models, allele frequencies, haplotypes, and phylogenetic relationships. A population may therefore be described as having a certain “Eurasian component” because its genome contains segments that statistically resemble a reference population. But resemblance does not automatically answer the question: **Who contributed the ancestry to whom?** Imagine an ancestral population X that existed before modern geographic population boundaries had formed. Population X later separates into populations A and B. Population A eventually becomes associated with Africa. Population B eventually becomes associated with Eurasia. Thousands of years later, a genetic analysis compares A and B. A may resemble B. But that does not mean that A necessarily received ancestry from B. Both could instead descend from X. This distinction becomes increasingly important as the time depth of the proposed relationship increases. --- # 3. Africa Is Not a Genetically Isolated Starting Point The conventional phrase “Out of Africa” can accidentally encourage an oversimplified mental picture. It does not mean that there was one African population sitting in one location from which all non-Africans suddenly emerged. Africa itself contained—and contains—enormous population structure. Ancient populations moved across the continent. Populations expanded and contracted. Climate repeatedly transformed the Sahara. The Green Sahara created ecological corridors across regions that are deserts today. The Nile Valley connected northeastern Africa with surrounding regions. The Red Sea connected northeastern Africa with Arabia. The Mediterranean connected North Africa with southern Europe and the Levant. Thus, the appropriate historical unit may not be “Africa versus Eurasia.” It may instead be: > **a constantly changing network of African and Eurasian populations connected by periods of separation, migration, and gene flow.** --- # 4. The North African Case North Africa provides an especially important test. A 2025 *Scientific Reports* study analyzing hundreds of modern and ancient mitogenomes concluded that modern North African mitochondrial diversity reflects contributions associated with Eurasia, North Africa, and sub-Saharan Africa, accumulated during multiple periods. The study also identified evidence for movements from sub-Saharan Africa into North Africa during the Green Sahara period. That result is more complicated than a simple: > Africa → Eurasia → North Africa model. It suggests repeated movements in multiple directions. More importantly, the same study identifies a distinctive North African component associated with ancient lineages such as U6 and M1 and discusses prehistoric movements back into Africa. The important methodological question therefore becomes: > When researchers call a component “Eurasian,” are they identifying its immediate source population, its ultimate evolutionary origin, or merely the modern reference population to which it is most genetically similar? Those are three different things. --- # 5. The Amazigh Question Amazigh populations are particularly interesting because North Africa preserves an extremely deep human history. Ancient DNA research has identified a distinctive North African ancestry component in Late Pleistocene individuals from Morocco. A 2025 *Nature* study of approximately 7,000-year-old individuals from the Central Sahara found a previously unknown North African lineage that had diverged from sub-Saharan African lineages around the same broad period as the divergence associated with present-day humans outside Africa. The study also found close relationships between these Central Saharan individuals and ancestry previously identified in approximately 15,000-year-old Moroccan foragers. This raises an important question: > **Could some ancestry labeled “Eurasian” in present-day North Africans partly reflect ancient population structure that predates the later formation of the modern African–Eurasian genetic divide?** That is a testable question. It should not be answered by terminology alone. --- # 6. The Egyptian Question Egypt provides an even more complicated case because it sits at the intersection of Africa, the Levant, the Mediterranean, and the Red Sea. A 2025 *Nature* study produced whole-genome data from an approximately 4,500-year-old Egyptian individual from Nuwayrat. Most of this individual's ancestry was best represented by North African Neolithic ancestry. The study also found that many present-day Egyptian genomes can be modeled using combinations involving North African, Levantine, Ethiopian, and Central/West African-related sources, while emphasizing the limitations imposed by the currently sparse ancient-DNA record. This is precisely where terminology becomes important. If a modern Egyptian genome contains ancestry that is statistically related to an ancient Levantine population, there are several possible historical questions: **Did Levantine people migrate into Egypt?** **Did Egyptians migrate into the Levant?** **Did both populations inherit the ancestry from an older population?** **Did repeated migration in both directions occur?** **Did a geographically intermediate population contribute to both?** A sophisticated genetic model must distinguish among these possibilities. --- # 7. The Horn of Africa The same methodological problem applies to Ethiopia, Eritrea, and Somalia. Research has detected ancestry related to West Eurasian populations in parts of the Horn of Africa. For example, genomic studies of Ethiopians have identified a non-African-related component and attempted to identify its closest ancient sources. One study specifically emphasized that a deeply divergent indigenous African component makes interpretation of the Eurasian-related component difficult and investigated different possible West Asian sources. This is important because it demonstrates that even researchers studying “Eurasian ancestry” must distinguish among multiple possible sources and demographic histories. The relevant question is therefore not simply: > “Do Ethiopians have Eurasian DNA?” The scientifically more useful question is: > **Which ancient population contributed the relevant genetic segments, when did that contribution occur, and what evidence establishes the direction of gene flow?** --- # 8. The Fulani Question The Fulani present a different problem. Their historical distribution extends across large portions of West and West-Central Africa, and their population history includes interactions with multiple African populations. Any apparent “Eurasian” component in Fulani genomes therefore needs to be analyzed against: * North African populations; * Sahelian populations; * West African populations; * ancient North African populations; * Near Eastern populations; * ancient Levantine populations; * Iberian populations; * and other potential source populations. A genetic component being closest to one modern Eurasian reference population does not necessarily mean that population was the direct historical donor. The intermediate population may no longer exist. --- # 9. The Central Logical Problem Consider the following simplified scenario. An ancient African population exists 100,000 years ago. It splits into Population A and Population B. Population A remains primarily in Africa. Population B participates in migrations that eventually populate Eurasia. Population B subsequently develops substantial genetic differentiation. Thousands of years later, descendants of Population A and Population B come back into contact. A modern African population is now genetically similar to a Eurasian population. A contemporary ancestry algorithm might identify: > “West Eurasian-related ancestry.” But the deeper evolutionary relationship could actually be: > Ancient African population → divergence → African lineage + Eurasian lineage → later contact. The ancestry is “Eurasian-related” in a statistical sense. It is not necessarily correct to interpret that label as: > “This African population recently descended from Eurasians.” --- # 10. How Can Genetic Testing Determine Direction? This is the central methodological challenge. Geneticists do have methods for inferring directionality. These include: ### Ancient DNA Ancient genomes can establish whether a genetic component existed in a region before or after another population's appearance. ### Haplotype length Recent admixture tends to produce longer inherited genomic segments, which are progressively broken up by recombination over generations. ### Coalescent modeling Researchers can estimate population divergence and migration parameters from genome-wide variation. ### Derived allele sharing Methods such as D-statistics and related tests can identify asymmetric allele-sharing patterns. ### Uniparental lineages Mitochondrial DNA and Y chromosomes can sometimes reveal demographic movements, although they represent only small portions of the genome. ### Temporal sampling Perhaps the most powerful evidence is chronological: > Population X exists at time T1. > Population Y appears with X-related ancestry at time T2. This provides evidence that X could have contributed to Y. But even here, the absence of older samples from the supposed source region can create uncertainty. --- # 11. The Reference-Population Problem There is another issue worth examining. Genetic ancestry percentages are not natural quantities like temperature or mass. They are outputs of statistical models. If an algorithm has reference populations A, B, C, and D, it attempts to explain an individual's genome using those references. Change the reference populations and the resulting ancestry proportions can change. Therefore: > **“30% Eurasian” does not mean that 30% of a person's genome carries a molecule labeled Eurasian.** It means that, under a particular model and reference framework, approximately that proportion of the genome is statistically better explained by ancestry related to selected Eurasian reference populations. That distinction should be communicated much more clearly. --- # 12. The Directionality Experiment A powerful research program could deliberately reverse the question. Instead of asking: > “How much Eurasian ancestry exists in African populations?” ask: > **“How much African ancestry exists in the populations being used as Eurasian references?”** Then ask: > **“Which population contains the older version of the relevant genetic variation?”** Then: > **“Where are the earliest ancient genomes carrying the relevant haplotypes?”** And finally: > **“What demographic model best explains the entire pattern?”** This changes the analytical frame from: **African population ← Eurasian ancestry** to: **Ancient population → population divergence → subsequent African/Eurasian populations → later reciprocal gene flow.** That is a fundamentally different question. --- # 13. The Most Important Test The strongest test would compare ancient genomes across time. For example: **African Middle/Late Pleistocene populations** ↓ **Early North African populations** ↓ **Early Near Eastern populations** ↓ **Early European populations** ↓ **Later African populations** ↓ **Modern populations** If a genetic lineage appears first in Africa and subsequently appears in Eurasia, the evidence for African ancestry becomes stronger. If it appears first in Eurasia and subsequently appears in Africa, the evidence for Eurasian-to-African migration becomes stronger. If it appears approximately simultaneously in multiple regions, a structured ancestral population or network model may be more appropriate. --- # 14. A More Precise Vocabulary This paper therefore proposes replacing several potentially misleading expressions. Instead of: > “X population has Eurasian DNA.” Prefer: > “X population contains genetic ancestry that is statistically related to sampled Eurasian populations.” Instead of: > “Eurasian ancestry entered Africa.” Prefer: > “The data are consistent with gene flow from a population related to the sampled Eurasian sources into the African population.” Then ask: > “What independent evidence establishes the direction and timing of that gene flow?” This is not merely semantic. It changes the hypothesis being tested. --- # 15. The Larger Evolutionary Question The deepest question is not whether modern Amazigh, Egyptian, Fulani, Somali, Ethiopian, or Eritrean populations have genetic relationships with populations outside Africa. They clearly do. Human populations have been connected for tens of thousands of years. The deeper question is: > **When genetic ancestry crosses the boundaries that modern researchers call “Africa” and “Eurasia,” which geographic label should be assigned to the ancestral population from which that ancestry ultimately originated?** That question cannot be answered simply by looking at where the closest modern reference population lives. The ultimate answer requires chronology. --- # 16. A Revised Research Hypothesis The hypothesis proposed here is therefore: > **Some genetic components currently described as “Eurasian ancestry” in African populations may represent ancestry inherited through complex prehistoric population networks whose ultimate origins cannot be established solely from modern reference-population similarity.** This hypothesis does **not** claim that Eurasian-to-African migration did not occur. It does not deny documented back-migration. It does not claim that every “Eurasian” component in an African population originated in Africa. Instead, it demands that directionality be demonstrated rather than assumed. --- # 17. Conclusion Humanity's deep evolutionary history begins in Africa, but the subsequent history of African and Eurasian populations is not a simple one-way arrow. It is a branching and reconnecting network. For populations such as Amazighs, Egyptians, Fulani, Somalis, Ethiopians, and Eritreans, genetic ancestry can reflect multiple periods of population movement across North Africa, the Sahara, the Nile Valley, the Red Sea, Arabia, the Mediterranean, and the Near East. Modern studies provide evidence for genuine Eurasian-related ancestry and prehistoric back-migration into Africa. But they also increasingly reveal deeply divergent African lineages and complex population structures that make a simple Africa-versus-Eurasia model inadequate. The provocative question is therefore not: > **“Do Africans have Eurasian DNA?”** It is: > **“When an African genome resembles a Eurasian reference genome, what evidence demonstrates that the relevant ancestry traveled from Eurasia into Africa rather than representing shared ancestry inherited from populations that existed before the modern African–Eurasian genetic distinction?”** And an even deeper question follows: > **“Should genetic ancestry be named according to where a related population lives today, or according to where the ancestral population that generated the genetic relationship actually lived?”** Those questions are empirically testable. The answer should come from ancient genomes, archaeological chronology, population-genetic modeling, and independently replicated evidence—not from geographic labels attached to statistical clusters.
