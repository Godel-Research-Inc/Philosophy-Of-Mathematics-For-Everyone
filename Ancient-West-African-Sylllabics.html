The Ancient Script Ancestors Hypothesis A Comparative Research Framework for West African Writing Systems and Proposed Relationships with Greek and Latin Academic-style hypothesis paper • October 2026 Abstract This paper proposes a testable research framework for investigating whether structural, graphic, or historical relationships may exist among West African writing systems—including Bamum, N’Ko, and Adlam—and older writing traditions associated with the Mediterranean. The hypothesis is intentionally presented as a research question rather than an established historical conclusion. Existing scholarship documents Bamum as a writing system developed under Sultan Ibrahim Njoya between 1896 and 1918, N’Ko as a twentieth-century script created by Solomana Kanté, and Adlam as a modern script developed for Fulani. Scholarly work on West African script invention also emphasizes local creativity, indigenous graphic traditions, and selective influence from scripts such as Arabic and Roman. The central contribution proposed here is a comparative methodology capable of distinguishing common ancestry from independent invention, borrowing, typological convergence, and later cultural contact. 1. Research Question Can systematic linguistic, paleographic, archaeological, and historical evidence demonstrate that apparently related features among selected West African scripts and Mediterranean scripts derive from a demonstrable earlier writing tradition? If so, what intermediate stages, dates, geographic pathways, and sound-symbol correspondences would be required to establish that relationship? 2. The Proposed Ancient Script Ancestor Model The hypothesis can be represented as a branching model: an as-yet-unidentified ancestral or proto-writing tradition → regional West African graphic traditions → later independent or partially connected script innovations. A stronger version of the hypothesis would additionally posit a historically demonstrable connection between that West African tradition and Mediterranean writing traditions. Such a connection should not be assumed merely because signs appear visually similar. 4. Competing Explanations At least four explanations must be tested against the same evidence: (1) common ancestry; (2) direct or indirect borrowing; (3) independent invention; and (4) convergence caused by shared constraints of writing, language, or visual design. Piers Kelly's comparative work on West African scripts argues that local pictorial traditions and annotation practices may have played important roles in script invention, while also documenting inspiration from Roman and Arabic systems in particular cases. ncitenturn0search1n 6. Greek and Latin as Comparison Sets Greek and Latin should be treated as comparison sets rather than assumed ancestors. A valid genealogical argument would require more than resemblance: it would require chronologically ordered intermediate evidence and systematic correspondences. The study should therefore compare the complete inventories, not selected signs chosen because they look alike. 7. Falsifiable Predictions A strong version of the hypothesis makes predictions. It predicts that additional intermediate forms should exist; that proposed correspondences should remain stable across independent samples; that chronological ordering should be compatible with transmission; and that similarities should exceed what would be expected from independent invention. Conversely, discovery that the strongest correspondences are late, isolated, geographically implausible, or inconsistent in sound value would weaken the common-ancestor model. 8.
