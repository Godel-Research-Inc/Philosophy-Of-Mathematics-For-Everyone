# Graham 𐒨⫰⟋ᔕ⟋ᗑ ### The Philosophy of Mathematical Pattern Discovery **Graham 𐒨⫰⟋ᔕ⟋ᗑ** begins with: > **Mathematics is the art of noticing a pattern before knowing why the pattern exists.** Its central question is: $$ \boxed{ \text{What hidden structure is generating the observations?} } $$ The philosophy has five primitives: $$ \boxed{ \text{Observe} \rightarrow \text{Connect} \rightarrow \text{Transform} \rightarrow \text{Constrain} \rightarrow \text{Abstract}. } $$ --- # I. 𐒨 — Observation Begin with examples: $$ x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots,x_n. $$ Do not immediately assume a theory. First ask: $$ \boxed{ \text{What repeats?} } $$ Patterns may appear as: * numerical regularities, * geometric structures, * symmetries, * sequences, * recurrences, * graphs, * probabilistic behavior. Observation is the raw material of conjecture. --- # II. ⫰ — Connection Separate observations become interesting when relationships appear. Suppose: $$ x_i\leftrightarrow x_j. $$ A collection becomes a relational structure: $$ G=(V,E). $$ The mathematician searches for: $$ E^\ast $$ —the hidden relationship that explains the examples. Thus: $$ \boxed{ \text{Pattern} = \text{repeated relationship}. } $$ --- # III. ⟋ — Transformation A pattern becomes more powerful when it survives transformation. Suppose: $$ T(x) $$ changes an object. If: $$ P(x)=P(T(x)), $$ then the pattern may be structural rather than accidental. This leads to: $$ \boxed{ \text{Transformation} \rightarrow \text{Invariant} \rightarrow \text{Theory}. } $$ --- # IV. ᔕ — Constraint A conjecture becomes meaningful when it rules out possibilities. Suppose we hypothesize: $$ P(x). $$ Then: $$ P(x) $$ restricts the space of possible objects. A strong mathematical pattern therefore has predictive power: $$ \boxed{ \text{Pattern} = \text{regularity that constrains future examples}. } $$ --- # V. ⟋ — Recurrence Patterns frequently reveal themselves through repetition. A sequence may satisfy: $$ x_{n+1}=F(x_n). $$ Then the infinite sequence: $$ x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots $$ can be generated from a compact rule. This gives the fundamental compression: $$ \boxed{ \text{Many observations} \rightarrow \text{one generating rule}. } $$ --- # VI. ᗑ — Abstraction The final step is to remove the accidental details. Suppose examples: $$ X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n $$ all exhibit structure: $$ S. $$ Then formulate: $$ A(X_i)=S. $$ The individual examples become instances of a general concept. Thus: $$ \boxed{ \text{Example} \rightarrow \text{Pattern} \rightarrow \text{Abstraction}. } $$ --- # VII. Conjecture Pattern recognition produces hypotheses. Given observations: $$ D=\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\}, $$ construct: $$ H(D). $$ The conjecture predicts: $$ H(x_{n+1}). $$ The next observation then tests it. Therefore: $$ \boxed{ \text{Conjecture} = \text{compressed prediction generated from observed structure}. } $$ --- # VI
