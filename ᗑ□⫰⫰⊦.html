# Morris ᗑ□⫰⫰⊦ ### The Philosophy of Mathematical Abstraction **Morris ᗑ□⫰⫰⊦** begins with a simple proposition: > **A mathematical object becomes understandable when its essential structure can be separated from the details of its particular representation.** Its fundamental question is: $$ \boxed{ \text{What information can be discarded without destroying structure?} } $$ Its four primitives are: $$ \boxed{ \text{Abstract} \rightarrow \text{Structure} \rightarrow \text{Compose} \rightarrow \text{Generalize}. } $$ --- # I. ᗑ — Abstraction Begin with a complicated object: $$ X. $$ Construct a map: $$ A:X\rightarrow\mathcal A $$ that removes irrelevant detail while preserving relevant structure. The resulting object: $$ A(X) $$ is an abstraction. Thus: $$ \boxed{ \text{Abstraction} = \text{loss of irrelevant detail with preservation of relevant relationships}. } $$ --- # II. □ — Formal Boundary The square represents the abstract object as a formal system. Once the irrelevant details have been removed, we define: $$ \mathcal S=(O,R,F) $$ where: * \(O\) = objects, * \(R\) = relations, * \(F\) = operations. The question becomes: $$ \boxed{ \text{Which relationships completely characterize the structure?} } $$ --- # III. ⫰ — Composition Abstract structures become powerful when they can be composed. If: $$ A:X\rightarrow Y $$ and: $$ B:Y\rightarrow Z, $$ then: $$ B\circ A:X\rightarrow Z. $$ The intermediate implementation can disappear from view. Therefore: $$ \boxed{ \text{Composition allows complex systems to be understood through interfaces}. } $$ --- # IV. ⫰ — Generalization The second \(\⫰\) represents repetition across different domains. Suppose structures: $$ X_1,X_2,X_3 $$ appear unrelated. If all share: $$ S, $$ then construct: $$ A(X_i)=S. $$ The apparently different objects become instances of one abstract pattern. Thus: $$ \boxed{ \text{Generalization} = \text{discovering one structure beneath many examples}. } $$ --- # V. ⊦ — Formal Preservation The turnstile represents the requirement that abstraction preserve the properties we care about. If: $$ X\models P, $$ we want the abstraction to retain the relevant consequence: $$ A(X)\models P'. $$ An abstraction is therefore not arbitrary simplification. It must preserve mathematical meaning. $$ \boxed{ \text{Good abstraction} = \text{compression + structural preservation}. } $$ --- # VI. Hierarchy Morris mathematics organizes abstractions into layers: $$ L_0 \rightarrow L_1 \rightarrow L_2 \rightarrow L_3 \rightarrow\cdots $$ For example: $$ \text{numbers} \rightarrow \text{algebra} \rightarrow \text{structures} \rightarrow \text{categories} \rightarrow \text{meta-structures}. $$ Each layer hides details of the layer below. Therefore: $$ \boxed{ \text{Mathematical progress} = \text{construction of increasingly powerful abstractions}. } $$ --- # VII. The Abstraction Function Let: $$ A_i:X\rightarrow S_i $$ be a family of abstractions. A useful abstraction minimizes unnecessary information while retaining a desired property \(P\): $$ A^\ast = \arg\min_A \operatorname{Complexity}(A) $$ subject to: $$ A(X)\models P. $$ This makes abstraction itself an optimization problem. --- # VIII. The Interface Principle Two systems may be completely different internally: $$ X_1\neq X_2. $$ Yet if they expose the same abstract interface: $$ I(X_1)=I(X_2), $$ they can be treated identically at the higher level. Thus: $$ \boxed{ \text{Different implementation} \not\Rightarrow \text{different mathematics}. } $$ The interface becomes the object of study. --- # IX. Invariants The most important abstractions often arise from invariants. Suppose a transformation: $$ T:X\rightarrow X $$ changes the representation but preserves: $$ P(X)=P(T(X)). $$ Then \(P\) is an invariant. Therefore: $$ \boxed{ \text{An invariant is an abstraction of everything that changes away}. } $$ This connects Morris directly to symmetry, topology, algebra, and computation. --- # X.
